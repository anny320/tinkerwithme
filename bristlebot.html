Build a Bristlebot

Your first robot: a tiny machine that scoots across the table on a vibrating toothbrush head. No soldering, no code — just a motor, a battery and about five minutes.

⏱ 5 minutes 🔧 No soldering 💸 Pocket-money parts 👍 Total beginner

What you'll need

Tap each item as you gather it. Everything here costs very little and is easy to find.

  • Toothbrush headThe bristly end of any cheap toothbrush, cut off
  • Small vibration motorA pager / phone-buzzer motor (3V, with an offset weight)
  • Coin-cell batteryA 3V CR2032 — the flat, round kind
  • Double-sided foam tapeA small square to stack the parts
  • Scissors or snipsTo cut the brush head free
  • Googly eyes (optional)Purely for personality

Build it, step by step

Six quick steps from a pile of parts to a robot on the move.

Step 1

Cut off the brush head

Snip the head off a toothbrush so you're left with just the bristly block, about 3–4 cm long. Angle the cut slightly if you can — angled bristles help the bot pick a direction.

Tip: shorter is faster. A smaller, lighter head vibrates across the table more eagerly.

Step 2

Stick foam tape on top

Press a small square of double-sided foam tape onto the flat top of the brush head. This is the platform you'll stack the motor and battery onto — the foam also soaks up a little vibration so nothing rattles loose.

Step 3

Mount the motor

Press the vibration motor onto the foam tape, sitting flat across the top. The little off-centre weight on its shaft is what makes the whole thing shake when it spins — that shake is your bot's engine.

Tip: let the two thin motor wires poke up and to the side so you can reach them in the next step.

Step 4

Add the battery

Stack the coin-cell battery on top, flat side (the + side) facing up. A second strip of foam tape under it keeps it steady. You now have a neat little tower: bristles, motor, battery.

Step 5

Touch the wires to the battery

Hold one motor wire on the top (+) of the battery and the other on the bottom (−). The motor whirs to life. With a coin cell there's no polarity to worry about — if it doesn't spin, just swap the two wires.

Tip: a dab of tape over each wire holds the connection so your bot can run hands-free.

Step 6

Set it loose

Put your Bristlebot down on a smooth, hard surface and let go. It'll buzz, wobble, then scurry off in its own direction. Congratulations — you just built and powered your first robot.

To switch it off, simply peel one wire off the battery.

Why does it move?

The motor has a tiny weight bolted off-centre to its shaft. As the shaft spins, that lopsided weight throws the motor — and everything taped to it — into a fast, tight shimmy, thousands of times a minute.

Each shimmy lifts the bristles a hair off the table and nudges them forward. Because the bristles are springy and slightly angled, they flick back down pointing the same way — so instead of buzzing in place, the whole bot ratchets across the surface. It's the same trick that makes your phone "walk" across a desk when it vibrates.

Off-centre weightTurns smooth spinning into a shake.
Springy bristlesGrip, flick and release to create steps.
Angle & balanceDecide which way it heads and how fast.
More voltageFaster spin, stronger shake, quicker bot.

Make it your own

  • Trim or bend the bristles on one side to steer it in circles or figure-eights.
  • Add a dab of modelling clay to shift the balance and change its path.
  • Build two and race them down a cardboard track — first to the end wins.
  • Trap one inside a paper ring to watch it bounce around like a pinball.
  • Decorate with googly eyes, paper fins or a tiny flag — same physics, more character.

A quick word on safety

Coin-cell batteries are dangerous if swallowed — keep them well away from small children and pets, before and after the build. Disconnect a wire when you're done so the battery doesn't drain or warm up, and recycle flat batteries rather than binning them. The motor is harmless, but its spinning weight can catch loose hair, so tie it back.
